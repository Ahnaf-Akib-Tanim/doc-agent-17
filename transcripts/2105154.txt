AI:
Start from the retrieval unit rather than the index.

The assignment's real requirement is not "build a vector database." It is to turn the scans into
searchable evidence in which the retrieval unit corresponds to what a curator asks about: a table
row, a plate legend, or a paragraph.

Once that is the objective, Stage 2 and Stage 4 become easier to justify. Stage 2 must recover the
structure that tells you what belongs together. Stage 4 must preserve that structure in chunks and
metadata. Embedding and FAISS are then implementation choices for retrieving those units.

Your design table should therefore repeatedly answer three questions: what information does the stage
preserve, why does the corpus require it, and what failure or cost would cause you to change the design?

ME:
A1 proposed DocLayout-YOLO fine-tuned from DocLayNet. We do not have corpus-specific region labels,
and the corpus contains 1853–1945 scans, lithographic plates and tables where columns are defined by
alignment. I chose a heuristic: RLSA + connected components + measured grid recovery.

How do I make that choice look like an engineering decision rather than "we didn't have a GPU"?

AI:
Do not make hardware the primary justification. Make corpus mismatch the primary justification.

DocLayNet is designed around modern born-digital document layouts. Your corpus contains full-page
lithographic plates, old typography, irregular spacing and tables whose semantics are encoded by
alignment rather than necessarily by modern table borders. There is no natural DocLayNet class for
the specific plate/legend relationship you need.

Your heuristic also has three capabilities that are directly tied to the speciality:
1. it detects figures before line finding and masks them so plate texture does not become fake text;
2. it recovers column bounds before grouping blocks into reading order;
3. it recovers a table grid so Stage 4 can construct row-level retrieval units.

Then state the cost honestly. The heuristic can over-fire on numbered lists, and the thresholds are
corpus-derived rather than universally valid. If you later obtain a sufficiently large hand-labelled
region set, fine-tuning a detector becomes a reasonable alternative.

ME:
I had a table detector based on two persistent vertical whitespace corridors. It looked plausible on
one table, but ordinary justified prose from Lucas triggered it.

I measured line fill and internal gaps. Prose lines fill around 0.58–0.74 of their own span, while
table rows are around 0.25–0.54. In tables, 67–100% of rows have a wide internal gap; prose is around
0–52%.

I ended up with: table iff at least three rows, at least 60% gapped rows and mean fill <= 0.55.

AI:
That is a much stronger detector because the rule now has a measured separation between two classes.

The important part for the design document is not only the final threshold. Explain what each statistic
means.

Line fill measures how continuously ink occupies the line's horizontal span. Justified prose tends to
form a relatively continuous text line, while a row of separated table cells has larger internal
whitespace. The fraction of rows containing a wide gap adds a structural consistency condition so one
unusual prose line does not create a table.

The "at least three rows" condition prevents isolated line patterns from being promoted to a table.
The 60% gap condition says the behaviour must persist across the region rather than appearing once.
The 0.55 fill threshold is derived from the observed ranges rather than copied from a generic detector.

That also gives you a natural "what would change it": a new volume with a substantially different
typographic distribution would require recalibration on that volume, or eventually supervised region
labels.

ME:
After the change I get 151 table regions. The grid recovery usually produces a few rows and columns,
with a median around six rows by two columns.

I am worried about overfitting because we only have four volumes.

AI:
That concern should be included rather than hidden.

The correct mitigation is not to pretend the thresholds are universal. First, derive them from measured
corpus distributions. Second, evaluate them across all four volumes rather than one page. Third, keep
the thresholds centralized in configuration so a new volume can trigger recalibration without changing
the code.

Also remember that Stage 2 is not the final classifier. Its output is consumed by OCR and chunking.
The downstream evaluation tells you whether the structural errors matter for retrieval. A detector
that has a slightly imperfect region label but preserves the table grid may be more useful than a
generic detector that gives a visually plausible bounding box but loses column relationships.

ME:
Figure detection was much worse initially. The detector found almost no figures and the plate pages
were being split into seven or twelve fake text lines.

The fix was to detect figures first, on grayscale, using close(15)+open(9), requiring at least 5% page
area and at least 10% ink after morphology.

Why is grayscale so important when the reader itself uses Sauvola?

AI:
Because the two operations have different objectives.

Sauvola is a local thresholding method. It is useful when the foreground/background relationship varies
across the page and you need to preserve faint text. But lithographic plates are often defined by
global texture and contiguous dark regions. Local thresholding can break the texture into disconnected
pieces.

For figure detection, grayscale plus a global threshold and morphology preserves the large-scale
solidity that tells you "this is an image region." For OCR, Sauvola creates a reader image that improves
local contrast for text. There is no contradiction in using different representations.

In fact, using one representation for everything would be less principled. The pipeline has multiple
consumers with different invariants. Keep raw, clean and reader images separately, and document why
each exists.

ME:
There is another subtle reason for figure-first ordering. If I mask the figure before line grouping,
the caption below an embedded photograph is detected separately rather than being swallowed into the
figure blob.

AI:
Exactly. That gives you a nice structural argument for the ordering.

The figure mask prevents picture texture from contaminating line detection, while the caption remains
outside the mask and can become an independent text block. That is important because in A3 the agent
may need both the plate image and its legend/caption.

It also explains why "figure detection before blank filtering" is not merely an implementation detail.
The figure is evidence. A blank-page classifier that runs first can accidentally delete a low-ink plate
before the pipeline has recognized what it is.

ME:

open(path, "w")

AI:
That is exactly the kind of bug that deserves to be in the transcript.

Opening a file with "w" truncates it. Because the call is inside the page loop, every page deletes the
manifest produced by the previous page and writes its own regions. The program succeeds because the
file operation is valid. The resulting JSONL is syntactically correct, just semantically incomplete.

There are two clean fixes. Keep one write handle open across the entire loop, or accumulate all regions
and write once after processing. Given 3,626 regions, accumulate-then-write is perfectly reasonable
and makes the final output deterministic.

But the bigger lesson is to add an invariant check. A manifest should not merely exist and parse.
Check the count and perhaps the page coverage. Your final breakdown of 3,075 text, 204 heading,
196 figure and 151 table regions gives you a concrete sanity target.

ME:

I want to connect that to our testing philosophy. We have ruff/black, pytest and structure/contracts/
tools gates, but many of our dangerous failures are silent: a split leak, a deleted plate, a manifest
truncation, or a chunker exceeding its token budget.

AI:
That should be explicitly stated in the design section.

Traditional unit tests catch exceptions and local logic errors. Your pipeline also needs data invariants
because many failures produce perfectly valid files.

For example:
- every page ID must be unique;
- every image path must exist;
- doc_id must map to exactly one split;
- figure/table counts should be within plausible corpus distributions;
- regions should cover the expected page IDs;
- chunk size should obey the configured bound;

This is why data/validate.py asserts the >=300-page and >=60k-word floors as well as structural

ME:
Now Stage 4. The semantic chunker makes one unit per table row and carries the header into each row.
I want to explain why this is better than ordinary fixed-size chunking.

On the same page, structure mode produces 38 units averaging around 22 tokens. Fixed mode produces only
3 units around 170 tokens. A structure unit can directly answer "which value is in this row?" while
the fixed chunk flattens the grid.

AI:
That is the correct argument, but add the cost.

A semantic chunking strategy is valuable when the retrieval unit corresponds to a real information
object. For a table, a row plus its header is an information object. A fixed token window can split
a header from its values or combine several rows, forcing the answerer to reconstruct the table from
a flattened sequence.

However, semantic chunking can increase the number of near-duplicate candidates. Your later quarry
failure proves that cost. So the correct design statement is not "semantic chunks are always better."
It is "semantic chunks better preserve table semantics, but they increase candidate duplication and
therefore motivate caption propagation, hybrid retrieval and reranking in A3."

That tradeoff makes the design decision much more defensible.

ME:

logic carried an entire trailing paragraph into the next buffer.

I changed the overlap to word-based. After emitting a chunk, only the last 32 words are carried over.
The new maximum is 288.

AI:
That is a clean invariant.

If the target is 256 and the overlap is 32, the carry-over cannot exceed 32 words by construction.
Therefore the maximum expected chunk length is bounded at target + overlap, assuming the implementation
checks the next token before appending.

The useful test is not just "maximum happened to be 288 on this build." Add a test that constructs a
long trailing paragraph and verifies that the overlap itself remains bounded. That turns the observed
bug into a regression test.

Also preserve the chunking mode in meta.json because A3 evaluation labels will point at chunk IDs. If
you change the chunker and rebuild without changing the corpus/index metadata, you can invalidate the
meaning of those IDs.

Page -> Region -> OCR block -> semantic chunk -> embedding/index metadata.
Each stage must preserve the information required by the next one.

What I learned:
The knowledge base is not just an index. It is a sequence of information-preserving contracts. If
layout loses table columns, chunking cannot recover them. If chunking loses the caption, retrieval
cannot distinguish repeated tables. If provenance changes without versioning, A3 gold labels can point
to different chunks. The quality of the final agent therefore depends on preserving the right evidence
at every earlier stage.
