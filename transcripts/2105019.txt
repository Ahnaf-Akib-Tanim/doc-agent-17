ME:
I also moved the split assignment into loader.py. The split is assigned once at document level:
Lucas and Gilmore are train, Colbert is validation, and Leidy is test. Why is document-level splitting

AI:
Because page-level random splitting would be misleading for this corpus.

Historical monographs contain repeated typography, running headers, recurring table formats, repeated
specimen terminology and sometimes almost identical pages. If you randomly split pages, page 117 of
one document can be in training while pages 118 and 119 appear in test. The model or the heuristics
then encounter extremely similar structure on both sides of the evaluation boundary.

Document-level splitting makes the held-out volume a genuine distribution shift within the same domain.
Leidy is especially useful as the test document because it is the difficult volume: its kept pages
have the faintest ink distribution and it contains many plates. That makes the test slice useful for
the robustness question rather than merely proving that the cleanest volume can be processed.

Also, assigning the split in loader.py and nowhere else prevents downstream stages from inventing a
second source of truth. pages.jsonl should carry the assignment and every later artifact should inherit
it.

ME:
That leads to preprocessing. The obvious recipe would be "deskew every page, despeckle every page,
binarize every page." But our measured skew distribution is surprisingly small: median 0.00 degrees,
mean absolute skew 0.13, p90 0.25, and only around 1% of text pages exceed 0.5 degrees.

So I set a gate at |skew| > 0.5 degrees and never deskew plates. The implementation searches from
-3 to +3 degrees in 0.25-degree steps using horizontal projection-profile variance.

Is that a sufficiently strong justification, or should I just say deskew improves OCR?

AI:
Do not say the generic statement. Your measured distribution gives you a much stronger justification.

Your choice is not "deskew is good." It is "deskew is conditionally applied because 99% of relevant
text pages do not need it." For a scanned historical corpus, unnecessary resampling can blur small
strokes, alter character boundaries, and change the pixels that later serve as citation evidence.

The projection-profile search is also explainable. For a page containing horizontal text lines, the
correct rotation tends to increase the concentration of ink into horizontal bands. Maximizing the
variance of the horizontal projection therefore gives you a simple, label-free estimate of skew.

The 0.5-degree threshold should be described as a measured gate rather than a magic constant. You
measured the distribution first, then selected a threshold above the normal variation. The fact that
only about 1% of text pages cross it means the operation is targeted.

The plate exemption is equally important. A plate is not merely another text page. You need its exact
spatial content later, and unnecessary interpolation on a full-page lithograph can modify the source
pixels. So store raw and processed versions separately.

ME:
For despeckling I use a maximum connected-component size of 2 pixels and a 3-pixel proximity rule.
The idea is to remove isolated specks but preserve components that touch or lie close enough to a
real character stroke.

I initially implemented it with a loop over components and np.where(labels == label). It became very
slow on the full pages.

AI:
That is a classic image-processing performance trap. The semantic rule is fine, but the implementation
repeatedly scans the whole label image.

You can preserve the same rule while making it vectorized. Compute component areas once, identify the
small components, construct a mask of the larger components, dilate that mask by the proximity radius,
and then use the dilated mask to determine which small components are adjacent to meaningful content.
You can also use bincount over the connected-component labels rather than calling np.where for each
label.

More importantly, measure how much of the page you are removing. The metadata should contain something
like despeckled_frac so later you can correlate aggressive cleaning with OCR quality. If Gilmore has
a substantially higher speckle fraction than Lucas, that becomes part of your quality-band analysis.

Do not optimize away the audit trail. In document processing, a fast destructive transformation is
worse than a slow transparent one.

Now for Section 2. I want to explain why spatial position is actually semantic here. A number in a
table is meaningless if we lose which column it belongs to.

AI:
That is probably the clearest corpus-specific argument you can make.

You should distinguish recognition invariance from layout invariance. The recognizer should ideally
recognize the same glyph regardless of small translations. But the document representation cannot be
translation-invariant because absolute relative position within the table determines the semantic
role of the value.

So your architecture separates two jobs. OCR answers "what characters are present?" Layout answers
"where are those characters and how are they grouped?" Stage 4 then uses the layout structure to create
retrieval units that preserve the relationship between row labels, headers and values.

This is also why the table grid from Stage 2 must feed the chunker. If the grid is only used for a
visual diagram and discarded before indexing, the pipeline has recovered the very information the
retrieval system needs and then thrown it away.

The final decisions were still based on the repository's measurements, outputs and failure cases.

What I learned:
The most important lesson from Stage 1 is that a knowledge base for historical documents cannot be
treated as a simple OCR pipeline. The page's spatial structure is part of the information. Preserving
